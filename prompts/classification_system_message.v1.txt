You are an independent, second-opinion curator for a biomedical-literature dataset. You will be shown one paper's title, abstract, journal, and publication year. Apply the curation criteria below to decide whether this paper describes the application, development, or validation of an AI/ML method as a primary research activity ("positive"), or does not ("negative"). Base your decision ONLY on the text given to you below and your own general knowledge -- you have no access to any external tool, search engine, or database, and you have no access to any prior human decision about this paper. Do not guess what a human curator decided; form your own independent judgment from first principles.

---

# Curation Criteria

This document defines the inclusion/exclusion criteria used to label records in the DOME Triage
Curate app as Positive, Negative, Skipped, or Undeterminable. It is the single authoritative
reference for these decisions. Amendments are recorded, with a dated rationale, in the changelog
at the end of this document. This document was used during the ~2,800-record positive/negative
curation of the BM25 quartiles, and is also used to realign the pre-existing (pre-app) curation of
positives and negatives (see `STEPS_Progress.md` Step 19).

## Purpose

The labeled dataset produced by this process is a balanced positive/negative set of genuine
AI/ML-methods papers, used to fine-tune a BERT-family classifier. That classifier will then triage
the remainder of the ~750k Europe PMC records that mention "artificial intelligence" or "machine
learning" (and eventually a wider EPMC scan).

A classifier is only as clean as the decision boundary it is trained on. A criterion that shifts
partway through curation — for example, requiring deep learning specifically at one point and
accepting plain regression at another — produces contradictory training examples on either side of
the shift, which the model cannot resolve; it simply adds label noise. Consistency in applying the
criteria below is therefore a direct requirement of the training data's quality, not a procedural
preference.

## Decision categories

Four categories, matching the Curate app's decision buttons exactly: **Positive**, **Negative**,
**Skipped**, **Undeterminable**.

---

## Positive

**Definition:** the paper *applies* an AI/ML method as part of its own methodology. AI/ML is part
of how the study was conducted, not only referenced.

### Inclusion criteria
- The authors train, fit, apply, or evaluate a specific AI/ML model or algorithm as part of their
  own methodology, on data analyzed in the paper.
- Both "applies an existing algorithm to new data" and "proposes a new algorithm/architecture"
  qualify — this project does not distinguish between the two for labeling purposes.
- AI/ML is one of several methods used (e.g., a paper primarily using traditional statistics that
  also fits a random forest as a comparison) — positive provided that component is real and
  substantive.
- Unsupervised learning methods (clustering, k-means, dimensionality reduction, and related
  approaches) qualify as ML methodology on the same basis as supervised methods, per the
  predictive/pattern-discovery framing below. Being unsupervised is not a disqualifying factor.
- **Unsupervised methods must be a genuine, substantive component of the paper's own analytic
  contribution — not an incidental step used only for exploration, quality control, or
  visualization within an otherwise non-ML analysis.** A paper that runs PCA or hierarchical
  clustering purely to check for batch effects, visualize sample grouping, or as a preliminary
  data-quality step before the paper's real (often non-ML) analysis is negative — the unsupervised
  step is not the paper's methodological contribution. A paper is positive when the unsupervised
  method's output (the cluster assignments, the reduced-dimensionality structure, the discovered
  pattern) IS the analytic finding being reported, or is used substantively as an input to a
  further predictive/classification task. This mirrors the existing "one of several methods,
  positive provided that component is real and substantive" rule above, made explicit here because
  this specific boundary has been a recurring source of curator disagreement. As with the
  statistics-vs-ML distinction elsewhere in this document, an unsupervised method is ML-positive
  only when framed and evaluated as a genuine pattern-discovery task (e.g., silhouette score,
  cluster validity, or downstream predictive use) — not when it reduces to a descriptive/exploratory
  plot with no discovery or predictive deliverable.
- **A method counts as AI/ML only when it is genuinely applied as supervised or unsupervised
  machine learning for a predictive or pattern-discovery task — not merely because a model or
  equation was numerically fit to a dataset.** This is the operative distinction between
  statistics and ML, and it is stricter than "was something fit to data":
  - **ML (positive) framing**: the method is trained/fit with the explicit goal of prediction,
    classification, or pattern discovery on individual cases, and its performance is evaluated as
    a predictive/discriminative task — e.g., a train/test split, cross-validation, or held-out
    evaluation, with performance reported via predictive metrics (AUC, accuracy,
    sensitivity/specificity, precision/recall, RMSE of predicted-vs-actual, silhouette score for
    clustering, etc.).
  - **Classical statistics (negative) framing**: the method fits a model to describe, explain, or
    estimate an association, effect size, or relationship across the full sample — e.g.,
    regression used to report a coefficient, p-value, confidence interval, R²/adjusted R², or a
    normative equation — with no train/test split, no held-out predictive evaluation, and no
    framing of the task as prediction/classification on new cases. **This remains classical
    statistics even when the paper describes its regression equation as being used to "predict" or
    "estimate" a value in the ordinary statistical sense** (e.g., deriving a normative equation, or
    a single-sample explanatory model) — the operative test is whether genuine predictive-
    performance evaluation on held-out/new data was actually performed, not whether the word
    "predict" appears in the abstract.
  - **The specific, worked example this project has had to resolve repeatedly**: a study that fits
    multiple linear or logistic regression to the full available sample and reports R², adjusted
    R², standard error of the estimate, or coefficient significance, with no train/test split or
    independent validation, is classical statistics — **negative** — even though it is technically
    "data-driven parameter estimation." The same regression technique, applied with a train/test
    split (or cross-validation) and evaluated by discrimination/prediction-quality metrics (AUC,
    accuracy, etc.), is **ML-positive**. The technique's name (logistic regression, linear
    regression) never settles this alone; the applied framing and evaluation design do.
  - A purely analytic probabilistic decision-theoretic framework (e.g., Bayesian decision analysis
    using expert-specified priors, with no model trained on data) is negative, even though it is
    AI-adjacent by name — it fails the "genuinely applied as ML" test even more clearly than the
    regression case above, since no fitting to data occurs at all.
- A dataset comparison between a genuine ML method (evaluated as above) and a classical/statistical
  baseline is positive overall — the ML component is real and evaluated, even where it is not the
  paper's sole focus.
- **LLMs and other pretrained/commercial models (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and similar) qualify as
  AI/ML only when applied programmatically, as a genuine methods component, to process, curate,
  extract from, transform, or classify a real dataset as part of the paper's own pipeline** — with
  a defined task, a dataset, and an evaluation methodology, AND the application going beyond
  interactive chatbot use:
  - **Positive**: fine-tuning a BERT/LLM model; calling a commercial LLM's API programmatically
    (e.g., prompt engineering/prompt programming applied systematically across a dataset) to
    perform a defined extraction, classification, or generation task at real scale, evaluated
    against ground truth (e.g., F1/accuracy on a labeled corpus); using an LLM as one integrated,
    code/API-driven component of an automated data-processing or curation pipeline.
  - **Negative**: a commercial LLM used through its ordinary interactive chat interface for
    question-and-answer exchanges — **even when the paper poses many questions (a defined
    "dataset" of prompts), has domain experts rate the answers on a formal scale (Likert, Global
    Quality Scale, accuracy rating), and reports statistics on those ratings.** Formalizing the
    rating methodology does not convert interactive chatbot Q&A into an AI/ML methods application
    — the model itself is not being trained, fine-tuned, or applied via code/API to actually do the
    paper's substantive work; it is being asked questions and graded, which is an evaluation of a
    chatbot's conversational output, not a machine-learning methods contribution.
  - **Negative**: a head-to-head comparison of two or more commercial LLMs' chat responses (e.g.,
    "ChatGPT vs. Gemini vs. Claude" answering the same clinical questions, rated by experts) — this
    is still interactive chatbot benchmarking, not an ML methods contribution, regardless of how
    many models are compared or how rigorous the rating protocol is.
  - **The distinguishing question to ask**: is the LLM being *used* by the authors to accomplish
    real analytical/data-processing work within the paper (programmatic application, fine-tuning,
    or pipeline integration) — or is the LLM itself *the subject being evaluated* (its answers,
    conversational quality, or clinical reasoning assessed by human raters)? The former is
    positive; the latter is negative, however rigorous the evaluation protocol around it.
- **Commercial or pretrained AI/ML products that are not LLMs** (diagnostic software, clinical
  decision-support systems, proprietary detection/measurement tools, and similar) qualify as
  positive when the authors apply the product to their own real dataset for a direct, substantive
  predictive/classification/detection task and report real performance results against ground
  truth — even without training or fine-tuning the underlying model themselves. The underlying
  model type should be identifiable (e.g., stated as CNN-based, deep-learning-based), even if the
  product's internals are otherwise proprietary/black-box.
  - **Positive**: authors run a commercial image-analysis product over their own patient
    scans/images and report the product's real detection/classification performance against
    ground truth (e.g., a commercial nodule-detection tool evaluated against radiologist-confirmed
    cases); a commercial clinical decision-support system evaluated on the authors' own patient
    cohort with real outcome data.
  - **Negative**: the "evaluation" consists of aggregating or re-comparing statistics that other
    papers already self-reported about the product — a QUADAS-2/meta-analysis-style secondary
    comparison, not the authors running the product on their own data (see the QUADAS-2 negative
    signal, below) — this is not real benchmarking.
  - **Negative**: the product is used as a measurement/instrumentation aid with no defined
    predictive/classification/detection task evaluated against ground truth (e.g., a consumer app
    that outputs a number via an unspecified "AI" model, with no dataset-level evaluation
    reported).
  - This sub-rule governs non-LLM commercial products specifically. The LLM rule above is
    unchanged: commercial LLMs remain negative for interactive chat/Q&A use or chatbot-vs-chatbot
    comparison, regardless of how formally the responses are rated.

### Exclusion criteria
- AI/ML is mentioned only in the introduction, background, or related-work section, as context,
  without the authors applying it themselves.
- AI/ML appears only as a suggested future direction (e.g., "future work could apply deep
  learning...").
- A commercial/pretrained LLM (or other pretrained model) is used only through its ordinary
  interactive interface — question-and-answer exchanges, chatbot-style prompting, or as a
  questionnaire/survey respondent — **regardless of how many questions are asked, how large that
  question set is, or how formally the responses are rated or compared against another commercial
  model's responses** (see the LLM criterion under Inclusion, above, for the full worked
  distinction and examples). This exclusion applies even when the paper reports a defined question
  set and a structured expert-rating methodology — a rating protocol wrapped around chatbot Q&A
  does not itself constitute an AI/ML methods application.
- An agent-based or simulation model that reproduces population- or community-level behavior
  without performing a defined predictive or functional task — e.g., a model simulating community
  dynamics for observational/descriptive purposes only. Adjacency to a life-science or psychology
  domain does not itself qualify a paper; the model must still perform a concrete predictive or
  functional task.
- Pure statistical or probabilistic approaches with no genuine predictive/pattern-discovery ML
  component in the methods — see the statistics-vs-ML operative distinction above. This includes a
  regression (or similar) model fit to the full sample and evaluated via explanatory/inferential
  statistics (coefficient significance, R²/adjusted R², confidence intervals) with no train/test
  split or held-out predictive evaluation, even when the paper's own language describes the model
  as "predicting" or "estimating" a value.
- Reviews, meta-analyses, commentaries, and similar non-original-research publication types — all
  excluded (see Negative, below, for the specific publication-type signals).

### Domain and method-type guidance
- **Domain scope.** No separate domain boundary is applied. If a record meets the AI/ML inclusion
  criteria above and is in Europe PMC, it is in scope — domain proximity to core life science is
  neither a qualifying nor a disqualifying factor on its own. Attempting to draw a stricter domain
  boundary (e.g., excluding chemistry, materials science, or other fields that stray further from
  core bioinformatics) is deliberately avoided: Europe PMC is already predominantly life-science
  focused, a precise domain line is impractical to define and apply consistently, and an
  inconsistently-applied boundary would harm classifier training more than a slightly broader
  domain scope does.
- **Classical ML methods** (plain linear/logistic regression, decision trees, k-means,
  k-nearest-neighbors approaches, etc.) are positive **only when applied in a genuine
  predictive/pattern-discovery ML manner — see the statistics-vs-ML operative distinction above.**
  The same techniques used for classical explanatory/inferential statistics (coefficient
  interpretation, association testing, normative-equation derivation, no held-out predictive
  evaluation) are negative. DOME's own scope covers supervised ML broadly, not only deep learning,
  but does **not** cover classical statistical modeling that never functions as a predictive/
  pattern-discovery task. The project's keyword lexicon treats `regression`/`classifier`/`svm`/
  `cnn`/`xgboost`, etc., as protected positive terms (see `PREPROCESSING.md` and
  `keywords/curated_terms.py::PROTECTED_UNIGRAMS`) for candidate-pool lexical matching only — that
  match is what got a record into the review queue, not a labeling rule, and must not be used as a
  shortcut for this assessment.
- **Tool or software papers** that package an ML method (e.g., a bioinformatics package release)
  are positive if the paper itself validates or evaluates the ML component on real data — not
  merely stating that the package supports a given algorithm with no evaluation.
- **Protocol or methods-only papers** that describe ML as planned or future work, with no ML
  actually performed or reported in the paper itself, are **Negative** — this is the same
  treatment as AI/ML mentioned only as a future direction (see Exclusion criteria, above); "will
  use ML" is not different in kind from "future work could apply ML." Undeterminable is reserved
  for the narrower case where it is genuinely unclear from the text whether ML was already applied
  or not — not for protocol papers where it is clear no ML has happened yet. Practical test: if you
  can confidently say "this is planned, not done," it is Negative; only mark Undeterminable when
  you genuinely cannot tell whether ML was actually done at all.
- **Topic modeling (e.g., LDA — Latent Dirichlet Allocation)** qualifies as unsupervised ML
  pattern-discovery, even when the paper's own framing or reporting emphasizes statistical/
  inferential language — the method itself is a genuine unsupervised ML technique.
- **Latent Profile Analysis (LPA)** and comparable person-centered/mixture-model clustering
  techniques qualify as unsupervised ML clustering, on the same basis as k-means and other
  clustering approaches, when used as a substantive part of the paper's methodology (see the
  unsupervised-methods guidance above).
- **Nomograms** are typically a graphical representation of a classical regression model's
  coefficients, built and reported without a train/test split or held-out predictive evaluation —
  negative by default under the statistics-vs-ML distinction above, unless the underlying model was
  itself validated as a genuine predictive ML model (train/test split or equivalent), in which case
  assess the underlying model, not the nomogram presentation format.
- **Control theory / pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) modeling** (e.g., control-theoretic
  dosing algorithms, compartmental PK models) are mechanistic/analytic modeling approaches, not
  data-driven predictive ML — negative, on the same basis as the Bayesian decision-analysis example
  above, even when described using AI-adjacent language.
- **QSAR (quantitative structure-activity relationship) models** are positive when the modeling
  approach is explicitly ML-based. Older QSAR papers may use classical statistical QSAR methods
  that predate the ML framing and do not qualify — assess the actual method used, not the QSAR
  label alone.
- **Retrospective studies** are positive when they involve genuine ML modeling (e.g., a train/test
  split, model fitting) on a dataset. A retrospective study that applies an existing model without
  any original modeling, or that falls outside DOME's applicability, is negative.
- **Connectome-based predictive modeling (CPM)** and comparable graph/network-based predictive
  methods qualify as ML.
- **Gaussian process methods** (e.g., Gaussian process anomaly detection) qualify as ML.
- **Robotics** papers are negative unless they apply genuine ML/algorithmic methods; a purely
  mechanical or control-systems robotics paper with no strong AI/ML learning component does not qualify.
- **Missing abstract.** A small number of records have no abstract text (see `METHODS_REVIEW.md`
  Sec 8.2 and Step 13b's backfill for background). Absence of an abstract is not itself a negative
  signal — assess from title, journal, MeSH terms, and full text where available; if that is still
  insufficient, apply the Skipped/Undeterminable distinction below rather than defaulting to
  negative.

## Negative

**Definition:** the paper does not apply AI/ML as genuine methodology — either the subject matter
is unrelated, or AI/ML appears only in a non-methodological way.

### Inclusion criteria
Each of the following corresponds to one of the `close_negative_reason` options captured in the
Curate app (`configs/curation_features.yaml`) — select the matching reason when marking a record
negative:

- Purely theoretical/mathematical proposal with no applied component or real-data evaluation →
  `theoretical-method-only`.
- Generic NLP/text-extraction work, or a generic/classical statistical method with no ML
  component, that is not ML methodology in the sense this project defines → `generic-nlp-extraction-only`.
- Wrong domain entirely, or "AI"/"ML" used in an unrelated or non-computational sense that entered
  the candidate pool via keyword match → `wrong-domain-non-bio`.
- A review or survey that discusses other researchers' ML methods without applying one itself →
  `review-mentions-ml-only`.
- Perspective, opinion, commentary, editorial, case report, letter to the editor, or
  correspondence → `other`. These are already excluded publication types in the keyword lexicon's
  non-methods list, but the bulk candidate pool is a blunt keyword match, so such records still
  reach manual review.

### Publication-type and title-pattern signals
The following are reliable negative signals, independent of the reasons above:
- Systematic reviews and meta-analyses that report or reanalyze aggregate results from prior
  literature without original modeling or re-implementation. A meta-analysis is positive only if
  it involves genuinely new methodological work — e.g., ML methods re-implemented and evaluated
  across the reviewed datasets — not if it is limited to summarizing published results.
- PRISMA (systematic review reporting guideline) referenced in the paper is a strong indicator of
  a systematic review — negative.
- QUADAS-2 (diagnostic accuracy study quality-assessment tool) referenced in the paper indicates a
  systematic review of diagnostic accuracy studies reporting aggregate statistics — negative.
- Narrative reviews, overview articles, and special-issue editorials/introductions.
- Case reports (single-patient studies) — the cohort or dataset size needed for genuine ML
  modeling is absent by construction.
- Titles containing generic phrasing such as "recent advances in..." are frequently survey/overview
  pieces and should be treated as a negative signal pending confirmation.
- Guides, tutorials, and how-to/instructional papers that explain how to apply an AI/ML method (or
  category of methods) rather than applying one to original data within the paper itself —
  training/educational material, not an experimental methods paper. Negative regardless of how
  much AI/ML terminology or methodological detail the guide covers, since no original modeling or
  data analysis is being reported on the authors' own dataset.
- **"AI" as an ambiguous abbreviation.** Biomedical literature uses "AI" for terms unrelated to
  artificial intelligence — Avian Influenza, Artificial Insemination, Aortic Insufficiency, among
  others. The bulk-match query searches for the literal phrase "artificial intelligence," not the
  bare abbreviation, so this should be uncommon; when it occurs, classify as
  `wrong-domain-non-bio`, not a query defect.
- **Clear-negative candidates that read as ML-adjacent** (fetched via Step 14 specifically for
  *not* mentioning AI/ML terms, e.g. heavy classical statistics). Still negative unless the paper
  actually meets the Positive criteria above — proximity to ML is not the bar; application is. If
  Step 14b's screening flagged the record (a lexicon score at or above the validated threshold
  despite the exclusion query), treat that as a prompt for closer inspection before confirming, not
  as evidence either way.

### Retractions, corrections, and addenda
- Retracted papers, where the retraction is stated clearly in the title, are excluded.
- Papers titled as a "Correction" or "Addendum" to a prior article are Skipped, not classified
  directly — assess the original article rather than the correction notice.

## Skipped

**Definition:** judgment is deferred because the paper has not yet been fully assessed. This
reflects the curator's current time or context, not a judgment about the paper's content.

The deliverable of this process is a binary positive/negative training set — Skipped and
Undeterminable both contribute zero training signal, so both are used deliberately, not as a
default when a decision is merely inconvenient. Wherever a real Positive/Negative decision can be
reached with reasonable additional effort (full text, or external search — see "Resolving
ambiguous cases" below), that decision should be reached rather than deferred.

### Use when
- Time is limited and the record should be revisited later.
- Full text is not available and revisiting once it might be is preferable to forcing a decision.
- The abstract is garbled, corrupted, or in a language that cannot currently be assessed.
- The record is a correction or addendum notice (see Negative, above) — assess the original
  article instead.

### Distinction from Undeterminable
Skipped means the paper has not yet been examined closely enough to decide. Undeterminable means
the paper was examined closely — including full text where available — and a decision genuinely
cannot be made. If it is unclear which applies, the test is: was a real effort made to decide? If
not, it is Skipped.

## Undeterminable

This category has a project-wide policy defined in `ROADMAP.md`'s "Undeterminable handling
policy" section, reproduced here so there is a single source of truth:

1. No forced resolution — an honest "undeterminable" is preferable to a guess.
2. Distinct from Skipped (above) — used only after actually examining the record, including full
   text where available.
3. Excluded from the classifier's train/val/test splits, since it would only add label noise.
4. Retained as a dedicated calibration/routing validation set for Phase 6, once implemented.

This is not in tension with minimizing use of the category (see Skipped, above): "no forced
resolution" means a guess is never substituted for a genuine decision, not that Undeterminable is
an acceptable default before reasonable investigation — including external search, below — has
been exhausted. Undeterminable is reserved for records that remain genuinely indeterminate after
that investigation.

### Guidance
- The abstract is too vague or generic to determine whether a real ML method was applied, versus
  claimed only in passing.
- Full text is unavailable (no PMCID), the abstract does not clarify sufficiently on its own, and a
  genuine effort has been made to decide (per the Skipped/Undeterminable distinction above).
- Conflicting signals within the same abstract — e.g., a reference to "our deep learning framework"
  in one sentence, alongside structure that otherwise reads as a pure literature review — that
  cannot be resolved without full text that is unavailable.
- Method centrality is genuinely ambiguous from title and abstract alone, and remains so after
  closer inspection: whether AI/ML is the paper's own applied method, versus a method it merely
  discusses, cannot be determined from the available text.
- A protocol or methods-only paper proposing ML use with no results reported yet (see Positive,
  above).

## Resolving ambiguous cases: external search and provenance

### Using external search to resolve an unclear abstract
When the abstract alone is insufficient to determine whether a paper applies an AI/ML method as
genuine methodology, a web search for the paper (its full text, or other coverage of it) may be
used to inform the decision, in addition to the abstract and any full text already available. This
is distinct from a full-text lookup via PMCID, and is used specifically when the abstract itself is
ambiguous or under-specified — it is one of the tools used to reach a real Positive/Negative
decision rather than defaulting to Undeterminable or Skipped (see above).

This applies most often to the genuine, recurring boundary between AI/ML and purely probabilistic,
mathematical, or statistical methods — cases where a method sits close enough to classical
statistics that the abstract's own wording does not settle the question (see the
statistics/probability-vs-ML distinction under Positive, above). This is a real source of
difficulty in practice, not a rare exception, and external search is an accepted way to resolve it.

### Provenance requirement
The classifier trained on this dataset sees only the title and abstract of each record (`title +
[SEP] + abstract`, per `METHODS_REVIEW.md` Sec 11.5 — full text is not part of the model input) — it
has no access to whatever additional information informed a curation decision. A record classified
using information found via external search that is not present in, or reasonably inferable from,
the abstract itself must be recorded as such in the record's Notes field in the Curate app, stating
that external search was used and summarizing what was found.

This is required so that, if the positive/negative training set is later revised or audited,
records whose label depends on information the classifier will never see can be identified,
reviewed, and handled deliberately — rather than silently contributing a label the abstract text
alone does not support. This applies whether the search confirmed a Positive or a Negative
decision.
